Overview

Introducing Lil Gryphie

After running a few bake sales, and seeing other clubs do the same, I wanted a robotics-special fundraiser that would create excitement on campus.

A Lil Gryphie is a 3D-printed gotcha box or blind box toy I designed to fundraise for my robotics team. All the money made goes to financially supporting students in attending competition. I wanted the team to feel accessible to everyone, so fundraising and sponsorships was among our top priorities. Alongside being excited about the successful fundraiser, I had a lot of fun optimizing our development, toy creation, and packaging processes.

the question

“Can we creatively fundraise for competition?”

02

Process

How I approached it

  1. 01

    Understand

    After looking into Simiskis, I designed six poses for a cartoonish gryphon, inspired by student life. I tried making his globular body in SolidWorks, but the software didn't easily support modelling non-geometric shapes. Instead, we reached out to Hunter Marshall, a student (now a graduate) from Ontario Tech’s Game Development and Interactive Media program. We went back and forth a few times on how to make the gryphon as cute and realistic as possible for 3D printing. One of our mechanical leads at the time, Jacob Gomes, gave us advice on 3D printing. After we perfected the body, Hunter modelled the designed poses in Blender, and Jacob started test printing them.

    Alongside this, I knew we needed some way to make them blind boxes. I drew my original vision in my mind and worked with the at the time VP Social, Rodney Malaa, to design a repeatable pattern tied to the lil guy.

  2. 02

    Build and test

    We ran a soft-launch sale to gauge crowd interest, test the ideal sale price, and run the development process from start to finish. It produced more revenue than any of our other fundraisers, which really surprised us. Many buyers said we should raise the price because they’d buy them at a higher price, which helped us, though it was funny that they shared that thought with us.

    The biggest thing I noticed was how long it took to set up the surprise boxes. Each box took 5 minutes to paint and 3 minutes to tape (properly and nicely).

  3. 03

    Iterate

    After the soft launch, we increased the cost by one dollar. Several people said we could go higher, but since we’re selling to students on campus and all proceeds go to support fellow students, it didn’t feel right to raise the price further. It felt counterproductive to squeeze the same community we’re making the product for.

    A bigger change we implemented was the box setup. Ordering Black boxes costs 1/3 the price of a single gryphon, which cut our gains by a significant margin, so I didn’t want to do it. By chance, the team had recently acquired a Bambu H2D, and I realized its small cutting tool gave us the opportunity to design our own boxes. I had Jacob play around with the ideal box design, and he came back with what’s pictured below.

    The final workflow optimization change we implemented was getting actual washi tape. Since the tape on the boxes made the aesthetic, it seemed worth ordering custom washi tape, which cut out the 3 minutes spent gluing the box shut.

    For all future sales, this made our workflow so much better. The longest part of the process is gluing the boxes together, but it is much faster and more reliable than hand painting recycled boxes.
